# Cinematography reference

Optional creative direction for an original university lesson. Adapt the world, examples and camera to the topic; this is not a fixed storyboard or scene template.

- Build a coherent, tangible 3D world. A car on a track, a laboratory instrument, a cup filling or a mechanical system can make an abstract quantity visible. Place graphs and explanatory geometry in that same space when this helps understanding.
- Start with a recognizable everyday event, make the change measurable, then reveal the mathematics. Show what each symbol means before asking the viewer to read a formula. Use accurate values and an example the narration actually discusses.
- Give each shot a purpose: a dolly reveals scale, an orbit reveals spatial relationships, a tracking shot follows motion, a close-up isolates the changing quantity. A whip or match cut should connect the same object, shape or idea across shots. Avoid a camera that circles continuously without explaining anything.
- Land camera changes, object actions, graph construction and formula reveals on the corresponding spoken phrase. Use the supplied cue times and measured audio exactly. Plan a meaningful visual action roughly every 3–6 seconds, allowing a longer hold when the concept needs it. Action should clarify the explanation.
- Create real depth with foreground, subject and background, clear lighting, shadows and spatially separated objects. Graphs need readable axes, distinct curves and an obvious moving point or interval. Keep the mathematical relationships correct during every move.
- Use crisp dark-to-light changes when the explanation changes state. Black and white provide the base; lime and blue are restrained accents for the important quantity or relationship. Maintain text contrast on both backgrounds.
- Keep labels and formulas sparse, large and stable long enough to read. Use short phrases, generous margins and clean hierarchy. Avoid paragraph slides, floating decorations and labels that collide with objects or other text.
- Make transitions carry meaning: a real trajectory can become a graph, a measured interval can become a formula term, and a highlighted point can match into a close-up. Keep object identity and direction understandable across cuts.

Aim for an energetic film that makes the subject clearer. Choose a new spatial narrative for this lesson; cars, laboratories and graphs are available ideas, not required ingredients or a derivative-specific sequence.
